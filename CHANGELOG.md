# Changelog

Notable changes to this project, newest first. Format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/); versioning follows [Semantic Versioning](https://semver.org/).

Nothing is released yet. Until the first public build (Phase 6 — see [`Docs/PROJECT_PLAN.md`](Docs/PROJECT_PLAN.md) Section 6), everything lands under **Unreleased**, and the project stays at 0.x with no compatibility promise of any kind — including for the save format.

## [Unreleased]

### Added

- **The control system scans at a FIXED PERIOD, not once per rendered frame** (`ATUCoasterRide::SimStep`, `SimHz` = 240, an accumulator in `Tick`). It ran at frame rate until now, which was wrong twice over. **Unfaithful:** a PLC scans on a fixed period and does not scan faster because the graphics card is idle — so the ride's control system executed at 144 Hz on one machine and 40 Hz on another, and every rate inside it moved with that: edge detection, restraint travel, overlap ageing, drive ramps. The already-documented "a train can cross a sensor inside one scan" limitation was quietly **worse on a slow machine**, which is exactly backwards from how a limitation should behave. **And not reproducible:** two runs of the same session diverged on the first frame, so nothing could be recorded and replayed — and a fault-injection scenario that reproduces differently every run cannot prove anything to anybody, which makes this the piece everything else about replay is downstream of. On a backlog beyond `MaxStepsPerFrame` the time is **DROPPED rather than worked off**, and that is a safety call rather than a performance one: catching up means running the ride faster than real time, and a ride that fast-forwards through a hitch can skip a train past a block boundary — the single failure this whole layer exists to prevent. Reported as a **scan overrun**, which is what a real controller does with a missed deadline rather than making the time up. Shown in the maintenance view alongside the scan rate. It also brings the actor into line with the prototype suites, which have always stepped at 1/240 s: the canonical figures were never affected because they come from those, and it was only the thing you actually *played* that varied — so `test_twotrains.cpp`'s "line-for-line stand-in for the actor's tick" is now true by construction rather than aspiration.
- **The reset button is MONITORED, edge-triggered 0-1-0** (`FTrackDrives::ScanResetInput`). It must be seen released, then pressed, then released again, and **the release is what clears the stop** — a press-triggered reset with a held button is indistinguishable from a taped one for exactly as long as somebody holds it down. Same reasoning already accepted for the dispatch anti-tie-down, except a wedged dispatch only runs trains early where **a wedged reset clears the E-stop**, so the ride restarts itself the moment whatever tripped it stops being true; it is the more dangerous of the two by a distance. **The trip itself clears the seen-low latch**, so a button already held when the stop latches is not the leading edge of anything — without that the sequence could be satisfied by a release from before the fault, which is an operator resetting something else entirely. Asserted with a button held for ten thousand consecutive scans, which buys nothing at all. `[End]` is now bound on press *and* release, because a key bound only on press is a taped button. One hole found and closed while wiring it: a fault raised **between** the press and the release would have completed the sequence and cleared a stop nobody had acknowledged, so the release checks again and `AbortReset` throws the sequence away — the acknowledge-before-reset rule was leaking out through a gap in time.
- **Stop categories, which the E-stop could not previously express** (`FTrackDrives::EStopCategory`, IEC 60204-1). Cat 0 is immediate removal of power; **Cat 1 is a controlled stop with power RETAINED to achieve it and then removed** — and the second half is the half that gets dropped, because Cat 1 is not "a gentler stop", it is a controlled stop *followed by* removal. This was a gap rather than a wrong answer: the behaviour was "output to zero this frame", which is a Category 0, and nothing recorded whether that was a decision. **The operator's button is the only Cat 1 on the ride and every automatic trip is Cat 0**, which is a risk judgement and exactly what the standard leaves it to — a person pressing the button has decided to stop and nothing is known to be broken, whereas a signalling violation, a detection disagreement or a faulted drive often means *the thing being asked to perform a controlled stop is the thing that failed*. **The default argument is Cat 0**, the harder stop, because Cat 1 is strictly weaker and a call site that forgets to say must get the stronger one; defaulting the other way would let every future caller weaken the stop by omission. Two things keep Cat 1 from being a hole: **the brakes are fail-safe**, so a zone commanded to zero bites and removing power *applies* them rather than merely ceasing to push — de-energise to trip, which is the property that makes cutting everything a safe thing to do at all; and Cat 1 carries a **deadline** (`SetCat1DelaySeconds`, 5 s), because a real SS1 relay does not ask the drive whether it finished, it opens the contactor at the window's end regardless — without it a drive whose ramp would take 200 s, or one that faults mid-wind-down, keeps its output for ever behind a latched stop. `Output()` now guards on **power removed** rather than on *stopped*, which is what lets Cat 1 keep driving while it winds down; the old guarantee is intact, since Cat 0 removes power in the same statement that latches the stop and needs no tick. **Ramps default to off, so on every shipped preset the two categories are identical** — the circuit's three-train mid-lap trip measures exactly as before, and the existing drives suite passed unmodified, which is the evidence the default was chosen correctly. Five new assertions, including the 200 s drive that must still lose power at the deadline. The panel says which category is latched, and distinguishes `CAT1 STOPPING` from `CAT1` — an operator watching a train still moving after they hit the button needs to know that is the stop working rather than the stop failing.
- **The ride profile is judged against acceleration envelopes, not just reported** (`Prototypes/TrainPhysics/GEnvelope.h`). `RunRideProfile` already said "4.25 g at S=310 m"; this says whether that is *allowed*, against the duration-dependent envelopes of ASTM F2291 and EN 13814. **Report, never repair**, as everywhere else — a track outside an envelope is still buildable and still rideable, it is just told where and by how much. **The limit tables are UNVERIFIED and the header, the docs and the suite's own banner all say so**: they came from a research session rather than a copy of either standard, and both are paywalled. The code is deliberately indifferent to the numbers — bands are data, and swapping in a verified table is editing one function. What makes it an implementation rather than a lookup: **you cannot check a peak**, because a limit is a curve against *duration*, so the bands are stored inverted (*no run above 4 g may last longer than 1 s*) and the header works a case through both forms, since getting the inversion backwards silently passes 5.9 g held for three seconds. It **resamples onto a uniform time base first**, because `RunRideProfile` samples by arc length and at 1 m spacing a sample is 0.025 s apart at 40 m/s and 0.5 s apart at 2 m/s — durations measured on that grid measure the train's speed. It **filters at 5 Hz per F2137 with a Butterworth run forward then backward**, so there is zero phase lag: the product is "go and look at S = this", and a single-pass IIR reports the violation half a second downstream on innocent geometry. Measured — a one-sample 12 g integrator artefact reads 10.8 g unfiltered and is gone at 5 Hz. **An impact is not a short violation**: events under 0.20 s leave the pass/fail entirely, and are reported *only when that exemption is what saved them*, because a short excursion that would not have violated its band even if sustained is noise — getting that ordering wrong gave the worked layout seven "impacts" that were 1.02 g wobbles over the 1 g band, which is the resting value of every ride ever authored. **Combined loading is a normalised ellipse**, asserted with a case where all three axes pass individually and the rider is still outside it. **One event is one finding**, because the bands nest and a 5 g pull-out crosses four of them. **Not applied, and carried in the verdict rather than left in a comment**: push-pull has no reduction factor in the source and inventing one would be worse than omitting it, and EN measures nearer head level where this model computes felt G at the heartline.
- **The envelope suite asserts the train completed, and that assertion is why the rest means anything.** Its real-ride test reported "within envelope, zero findings" for its first three runs — on a train that crawled 46 m up a hill it could not climb and stopped, because the lift supplied 1.0 m/s² against a 23° grade needing 3.8. A stalled train produces a short, flat, entirely conformant profile and a clean verdict, and **judging a ride that did not happen is the one failure mode a conformance tool must not have**. The layout was rebuilt to run (406 m, 96 km/h, 40 m of drop, 2.64 g at the pull-out) and the test now asserts completion, top speed and that the pull-out actually pulled. Second thing the rebuild found: **pitch curvature is an angular rate**, so a segment ending at non-zero curvature is still pitching when the next begins — the first draft's hills climbed monotonically to 104 m and never came back down.
- **The control panel has an operator view and a maintenance view** (`ETUPanelView`; `[P]` cycles operator → maintenance → off). Started as a question about an "easy mode / expert mode" skinning layer and became something better on contact with what a real installation does: this is **not a difficulty setting and not one screen at two detail levels**, it is the split every ride already has, and honouring it makes the sim *more* faithful rather than less. An operator dispatches trains, a maintainer diagnoses machines, and **motor current belongs to exactly one of them** — a real dispatch console does not carry it, and putting it on both is as wrong as leaving it out of both. **The drives section is the one place the views genuinely disagree**: commanded against output against motor, plus torque, is how you separate a slipping tyre from a ramping one, and the operator gets one word instead — `RUNNING`, `RAMPING`, `FAULT`, `STOPPED`, every one a fact the drive holds about *itself* rather than an inference about a train, which a drive has no way to make. `RAMPING` is the state that only became sayable when a command stopped taking effect instantly, and it is the one that explains a dispatch refused on the pre-launch term. Maintenance also gets **the second detection method's own per-block counts**, drawn under the schematic through the same widened mapping so a number cannot sit under a block it does not describe, plus a `DETECTION` line stating the agreement — because **a cross-check never seen to agree is indistinguishable from one that is not running**, and until now the only evidence it was alive was the E-stop it had not yet tripped. On an open layout the line says there is no counter, rather than showing an empty row that reads as a dead instrument. The operator view omits that breakdown on purpose: a disagreement already trips the stop and names itself in the events, and there is nothing an operator can do with the per-block detail that the stop has not already done. **The rule that keeps the split honest is that a view may omit a fact and may never invent or soften one** — the four drive states are not a rounding of the numbers behind them. Cost was one branch per section, because both views read the same live data off the same walk; nothing is cached for either, and nothing is computed for a view it is not shown in.
- **`bStartsNewDevice` is hidden on unpowered track**, where its own documentation already said it does nothing. One line of `EditConditionHides`, matching `ZoneSpeed` beside it — a field visible where it has no effect is the small version of the same defect the rest of the segment struct is careful about.
- **The UI framework and design language are decided, in writing** (`Docs/UI_CONVENTIONS.md`). **UMG**, with custom Slate widgets only for the two things that are *drawn* rather than composed — the profile graph and the panel schematic. The framing "UMG or Slate" is misleading, because **UMG is Slate**: a `UButton` owns an `SButton`, so the choice is of authoring layer rather than renderer, and that is what makes it cheap to revisit one widget at a time instead of all ten. The usual case for Slate is a list of hundreds of rows and **this project does not have one** — 23 segments for 615 m, a few dozen for the circuit, and the only thing producing thousands is CSV import, which nobody edits. What decided it is iteration speed: Slate is a C++ compile for every padding change, and the runtime segment editor is the largest single piece of UI work on the board. The **argument against is recorded rather than lost** — Widget Blueprints are binary and do not diff, which for an MIT project taking contributions means the UI is the part nobody can review; the mitigation is that behaviour lives in C++ `UUserWidget` subclasses and the asset holds layout only. Settled in the same file, because the alternative is deciding each one ten times: a 1600×900 floor with the DPI curve **clamped near 1.5 so a 4K monitor shows more rows rather than bigger ones** (and no panel hardcoding a row count, or the clamp buys nothing); fixed layout, no docking, with the one restraint that keeps the option open — no panel hardcodes its own size; and units **always shown**, with authored fields in the model's own metres and degrees, never converted on the way in, exactly as the save format already works.
- **The ride-profile traces use a colourblind-safe palette.** Okabe–Ito, replacing green / orange / cyan / magenta — the green/orange pair was the common deuteranopia collision, which the ride-profile card had already flagged. Vertical G sky blue, lateral G orange, speed bluish green, roll rate reddish purple; Okabe–Ito's own blue is deliberately unused at about 3:1 against the background, too weak for a 2 px line. **The control panel keeps its red / green / amber**, and that is not an inconsistency: those were corrected against photographs of three real consoles, and changing them for accessibility would make the panel less faithful to the thing it is modelling. What makes them safe is the rule the panel already follows and every real console does — **hue is never the only channel**, so meaning is carried by a lamp's fixed position and its legend, and colour only reinforces it.
- **`ACKNOWLEDGE` and `RESET` are two different controls, in that order.** Every real console has them as two separate buttons, usually not even on the same coloured field, and the ordering between them is the entire point: acknowledging says *"I have seen this"*, resetting says *"I have dealt with it."* A fault is now raised **unacknowledged**; `[Home]` acknowledges it — which silences the alarm and fixes nothing, since the drive is still faulted — and a reset is **refused** until it has been. Without the ordering the two controls are the same button twice, and a reset nobody had to read first clears faults nobody knows about. Deliberately not bound to `[A]`, because that is strafe-left and acknowledging a fault every time somebody moved the free camera is exactly the accidental press the ordering exists to prevent; on a keyboard the physical separation a console uses cannot be meaningful, so the guard is in software instead.
- **Restraints are a commanded device, not a dwell timer** (`FCommandedBank`, one instance for the bars and one for the gates). On every real operator console `GATES` and `RESTRAINTS` are selector switches and `LOCK HARNESS` is a button that lights while it works: the operator **commands** them and sensors then **confirm**. A thing that has been told to close is not a thing that *has* closed — the same command → device → feedback shape the drives already have, and for the same reason. The bank carries a commanded position, a travel time, and **a count of separately-sensed groups** (a car, a row, a platform segment); locked means *every* group reports locked, ANDed, because a train with one bar open is a train with an open bar however many are shut. That makes **"commanded closed but car 3 is not locked" expressible**, which is the failure a walk-round exists to find and the one a single bool could not say — asserted with a stuck group holding the dispatch through a full minute of trying, where the old clock declared it shut a second and a half in. Travel time is **hardware rather than crew allowance**, so the securing step takes the longer of the walk-round and the bars: an operator cannot sign off bars that are still moving, however quickly they walk. The crew *owns* the bank rather than being handed one, so nothing that already called `Serve` changed. The panel shows the bank's own sensors — `HARNESS 3/4` in amber while bars travel, rather than a lamp that lies about a switch position. `RestraintTravelSeconds` and `RestraintGroups` are `EditAnywhere`; gates take half the travel time, since they carry nothing. **Gates got the same treatment in the same pass**, so the all-clear is two contacts and a walk-round rather than one contact and a clock — a jammed gate section holds the dispatch exactly as a stuck bar does, and has its own test because the two banks are commanded from adjacent lines and a copy-paste that ticked one and tested the other would pass every restraint assertion in the file.
- **Pre-launch: the device you are dispatched *into* must be ready, not merely clear.** The step a real console has between "everything is secured" and "you may go" — harness locked and gates closed → **COMPLETE** → **PRE-LAUNCH** → advance/dispatch. It belongs to the *device* rather than the platform: a launch armed and charged, a chain actually turning, tyres up to speed. So the permissive carries a third term, and it asks a genuinely different question from the first — a block with a launch in it can be perfectly empty and still refuse a train. **It needed nothing new to compute:** a drive is ready when its output has *reached* its command and nothing is faulted, and one still ramping is not. Those two states became distinguishable for free the moment a command stopped taking effect instantly, which is the whole reason `Commanded` and `Output` are two numbers — the drives layer paying off a second time. An E-stopped ride is ready for nothing, and **plain track is always ready**, since a term that denied a dispatch onto open course would stop the ride rather than protect it. Ramps default to off, so on the shipped presets a drive reaches its command in one frame and the term is satisfied immediately; every measured figure is unchanged. Tested by giving the launch a real 4 m/s² ramp and starting it from rest: the station refuses to dispatch for the 9.5 seconds it takes to reach 38 m/s, then allows it. The panel says `DISPATCH — PRE-LAUNCH` rather than `— block ahead`, because waiting for an occupied block and waiting for an unarmed launch are two different waits and an operator needs to know which.
- **The generated control panel** (`[P]` in play). The Phase 3 differentiator, and it needed no new simulation: everything it shows already existed. **Generated, not authored per coaster** — it walks the same ordered block and zone lists the geometry and the physics walk, and emits each element's control-room counterpart: a block becomes an indicator, a powered run a **VFD module** (commanded / output / motor / torque, with a fault flag), a platform a sequence readout with what is holding it. Add a block to a layout and an indicator appears, because there is nowhere else for it to come from. Every number is read live, so it cannot drift from the ride the way a second copy would. Drawn on the debug canvas rather than as a UMG widget — a generated panel has no fixed set of elements to lay out in an asset, and this way it needs no asset at all. **Then corrected against photographs of three real operator consoles**, which changed three things: the blocks became a **schematic strip** rather than a table, because every real HMI draws the track and puts the lamps on it; the panel gained the **green-operating / red-stop colour fields** all three use to keep a hand reaching for one nowhere near the other; and `ADVANCE` was separated from `DISPATCH`, which are different moves and separate labelled buttons on a real console — an advance shuffles a train up the platform with riders still boarding behind it, a dispatch is the last point anybody can change their mind. The model could already tell them apart. Confirmed by those photos rather than reasoned: `ADVANCE & DISPATCH` appears **twice on all three panels, at opposite ends**, which is the two-hand rule the anti-tie-down logic already assumed, and one HMI page reads `Unlock Seats Segment 1 / 2 / 3` — restraints released **per platform position**, independent confirmation that a multi-position platform is the normal shape rather than an exotic one. Block widths take a **minimum** rather than being strictly proportional, because four 10 m platform positions on a 1288 m circuit came to three pixels each: the most interesting part of the ride, invisible, while a 696 m free run took two-fifths of the panel to say nothing in. There is no yellow lockout band, because lockout is not modelled and a lamp for a control that does not exist would be the panel telling its first lie — `SIGNALLING.md` lists the rest of what these consoles have and this does not.
- **A second, independent means of knowing where the trains are — and a disagreement stops the ride.** `FBlockCounter` was proven equal to perfect knowledge in its own suite (one train, a synthetic ring, three laps) and then nothing read it: the interlocking went on being handed each train's exact span. Rather than rewrite the safety core to fix that, the counter now runs **alongside** it — same blocks, same frame, nothing in common but the answer. One knows where every train is because it is told; the other has nothing but rising and falling edges at the block boundaries. **The disagreement is the product:** two ways of knowing the same fact, derived from different information, so if they ever differ then one is wrong and neither can say which — which is precisely why a real installation pays for a *second detection method* rather than a better single one. A difference trips the emergency stop, as does a block counted **twice** (a collision, detected without anything ever having known a position) and a block counted **below zero** (not a collision but a *lie* — a missed trip or a bad seed, after which the counter cannot be trusted to detect anything). Seeded from where the trains actually are, which is the operator's sweep. **Circuits only:** `FBlockCounter` is a counter over a *ring*, and on a point-to-point layout the wrap is false — sensor 0 going low is a train being placed at the start, not one leaving the end. Measured on both circuits at every train count: the two agree on **every block on every frame**, and mutating the counter's falling-edge rule makes that assertion fail, so the check bites rather than passing vacuously. It also turns the sensor layer from a proven-equivalent curiosity into something the ride's safety rests on — the counter was verified once, on one layout; now every run of every layout checks it.
- **A fourth preset: `SmallBatch`** — the same closed oval, re-zoned for a small-batch operation. 6 m vehicles, an **unload platform and three loading positions**, so the ride runs a queue of trains through a station instead of one at a time. **Eight** places a train may be held against the two-train circuit's five, so it carries seven; five run it clean for ten minutes. **The geometry is identical on purpose, and derived rather than copied:** that oval closes to 0.000000 m because of its *leg lengths*, and leg A is 176 m of flat whether it is one 26 m station plus a 150 m launch or four short platforms plus a 136 m one. Keeping the total keeps the closure, and building it from `TwoTrainCircuitLayout()` means a copy cannot drift from the shape it depends on — a seam that misses by a metre looks fine from the cockpit and teleports the train once a lap. Asserted against the two-train layout: identical total length, C2, seam within 1e-3 m, and it still gets round on a 6 m train, which a shorter train is *not* guaranteed to do since less of it straddles each crest and it pays more of the height. **A preset now brings its train with it** (`ApplyPresetTrainSetup`, on explicit load only, so an author who has chosen a different train keeps it) — because the train is part of the worked example, and loading this layout with the 15 m train the others use is the bug below.
- **A device shorter than its train is now reported at build time.** The stop mark is surveyed at `max(deviceStart + trainLength, deviceEnd − clearance)`, and on a device too short for the train the first term wins and the mark lands **past the far end**, where nothing will ever trip it. A train sent to park there crawls straight out of its own block into the next one and collides with whatever is standing in it. Found the hard way while building the preset above — a 10 m platform position with the default 15 m train gave **seven signalling violations inside four seconds**, and the prototype's circuit builder had the same bug because it placed marks against a hardcoded length rather than the train's. Reported, not repaired, like every other authored-value check here: clamping the mark back inside would produce a device that stops a train with its nose hanging over the boundary, which is worse because it looks like it worked.
- **Multi-position platforms — three trains loading at once, dispatched individually.** Common on rides with small vehicles, where one train's worth of riders is a handful of people and the platform is long enough to work three at once. **Almost none of it needed writing**, which is the payoff of having made the station process one per *position*: the interlocking already says "advance when the space ahead frees", and each position already gates its own dispatch. There is no sequencing rule because a train cannot pass the train in front of it and the block system already knew that. Two things did need building. **`FTUTrackSegment::bStartsNewDevice`** — three loading positions are the same kind at the same speed, so neither run-splitting rule can tell them apart; they really are identical and are still three machines with three motors and three blocks. The same flag authors a queue of brake sections. And **"loaded" belongs to the train, not the platform**: riders board once, at whichever position their train was standing at, and the train then advances *full*. The first version of the test had every train re-run the whole boarding sequence at each position it passed through — a platform that can never empty. It is per-vehicle state the ride control keeps, exactly as a real PLC does, and the platform is *told* rather than deciding, because a switch has no idea which train is over it. A pass-through position still costs the operators' all-clear, since moving a train with people in it is a move somebody confirms. **Measured, and it is the operational argument for the whole shape:** with 8-second loads, departures at 19.2, 58.2 and 99.3 s; give one rider 52 extra seconds at the **rear** and it costs the ride **5.5 s** with the two trains in front unaffected *to the frame* (asserted exactly — "about the same" would pass on a mechanism that coupled them weakly, and the claim is that they are not coupled at all); the same delay at the **front** costs the full **52 s** and everybody pays it. Positions are three loading bays, not a queue for one, which is why an operation moves a slow load to the back of the platform when it can see one coming.
- **An emergency stop, and it lives inside the drives.** A real E-stop cuts power to the motors; it is not a request the control program is invited to honour. Implemented one layer up — as "the dispatcher also commands zero" — it would be a stop with a hole in it the width of every caller that forgot, and the one thing that must never have holes is the stop. So it overrides the **output** rather than the command: the PLC may go on asking for whatever it likes and nothing turns. **It does not stop trains, it stops the ride**, and that falls out of the model rather than being arranged — a train on open course has nothing touching it, so it coasts, runs to the next brake and is held there; a train already in a brake run stops at once, because a brake commanded to zero bites. Nothing had to special-case where anybody was. Measured on the closed circuit: tripped mid-lap with three trains spread around it, **every one comes to rest at a holding device**, no violation and no shared block on the way down — and that last part is a property of the *layout*, since a ride with a device within reach from anywhere is one that can be restarted rather than walked out to. **Latched, first reason wins, cleared only by a person**: a stop that cleared itself when the condition passed would be a stop nobody had looked at, and a trip that overwrote its own reason with whatever failed next would throw away the only thing worth knowing. A cut drive also stops accruing slip, or an E-stop would raise a drive fault on every loaded motor seconds later and fill the panel with the same failure. **Three conditions trip it automatically, and all three were already detected and only logged** — a signalling violation, a drive fault, and a block counted occupied twice. The drive itself still only *reports* a fault; deciding what a ride does about a failed motor is the PLC's job, and this is the PLC doing it.
- **Manual dispatch, with the one rule that matters about it.** `EDispatchMode` on the station process and `bDispatchRequest` alongside the other contacts. Manual changes **who decides the timing, never whether the safety logic can be bypassed** — the request is ANDed on the end of every other condition and never checked instead of one, asserted directly: a button held down from the moment a train arrives, with every step of the sequence incomplete, buys nothing at all. **A tied-down button dispatches nothing**: it must be released and pressed again for each train, so a wedged or taped control does not run a ride. Real panels go further and use two buttons far enough apart that one person cannot hold both; the release rule is the cheap half of the same idea and catches the same abuse. "Nobody has pressed it" and "it is being held from the last train" are reported differently, because they look identical on the wire and are a stuck operator versus a stuck button. Keys: `[Space]` dispatch, `[Backspace]` emergency stop, `[End]` reset.
- **A run ends where the device changes, and the device is its kind *and* its speed.** Kind alone was the original rule, so a brake run at 6 m/s followed immediately by one at 2 m/s became a **single zone targeting 6, with the 2 discarded** — a typed number the build threw away, which is exactly what this project treats as a defect everywhere else. It was logged as a warning and worked around by wedging a different zone kind between the two. Splitting on the speed as well is both the smaller rule and the honest one: two speeds is two devices, so two zones and two blocks. It is also what lets **several holding devices be authored in a row**, which is all a backstage buffer keeping trains fed to a platform ever was — brake sections with drive tyres, one train each, ten of them being ten ordinary blocks rather than anything the interlocking cannot express. Until this, ten in a row authored as one zone holding one train. Same kind *and* same speed still merges, which is the rule doing what it says: that really is one device spanning several segments, and it is how every lift hill in this project is authored. **No preset changed** — every run in all four is a single speed already, and every canonical figure the two-train circuit prints is identical.
- **A station is a process, and the dispatch permissive is now an AND** (`Prototypes/BlockSignal/StationProcess.h`). A train arrives, riders get off, riders get on, restraints are closed and checked, the platform is confirmed clear, and **only then** may a dispatch happen — the block signalling is the *last* link in that chain rather than the whole of it, and on a working ride it is usually the term that went green first while an operator was still walking the train. Before this a train left the instant the track ahead was free, which is a ride with nobody in it. **Every gate is a contact, not a timer:** the stop mark, a restraint lock sensor per car ANDed into one signal, airgate switches, the operators' all-clear. Minimum dwell is a throughput target rather than what gates the dispatch — model the gates and the dwell falls out; model the dwell and the gates never get built. `FAutoStationCrew` asserts those contacts on timers because nothing here simulates a person, and it is a **separate object precisely so it can be deleted**. **Readiness is continuous and the release is latched**, which reads like a contradiction and is not: every condition is re-derived each scan right up to the moment of release, so a restraint popping open in `Ready` takes the permission away the same frame — but once the train is moving there is no such thing as un-dispatching it. Leaving that second half out **deadlocked the circuit**, and the failure is worth keeping: a released train rolls off its stop mark, so it is no longer "in position", so the sequence is no longer complete, so the permissive drops, so the dispatcher re-commands the brake, and the train stops with its tail still over its own mark and sits there for the rest of the session with nothing reporting anything wrong — measured, the launch drive never saw a single train in seven minutes. **One process is one POSITION, not one platform**, so a three-position load platform is three of these in series rather than a rewrite. `ETUSegmentZone::StationUnload` and `StationLoad` cover a split operation, and the roles differ substantively: an unload platform must **not** wait for locked restraints, because the train runs empty to the load platform ready to board and nobody is aboard to close them. The four dwell figures are `EditAnywhere` under a Station category. Capacity is unchanged with dwell in the loop — four trains still run the circuit clean. Recorded alongside it, because it was nearly built as a feature it is not: **a backstage buffer keeping trains queued at the platform is brake sections with drive tyres**, one train each, which is a block brake and is already exactly what the model expresses. It is a layout decision for one particular ride rather than a missing concept. The only gap is authoring — a run is a contiguous stretch of the same *kind*, so ten block brakes in a row become one zone and one block, which is the same wart as the already-warned-about "speed change inside a run is discarded", and the fix belongs in how runs are split rather than in a new kind of track.
- **The rails are coloured by the device on them** — station dark blue, lift and launch green, brakes red, plain track white. Only the running rails take the colour; heartline, spine and ties keep their own, because a view where everything changes at once says less rather than more. It earns its keep because the devices are the part of a layout you *cannot* see: geometry is visible, a hill is a hill, but which stretch can hold a train, which can only slow one and which can only push one all look identical in wireframe, and confusing them is the most expensive authoring mistake this model allows.
- **`ETUSegmentZone::Station`, and it was a signalling defect rather than a colour.** The station was authored as a `Lift`, and a zone is a contiguous run of the SAME KIND — so on the reference and out-and-back layouts the station and the lift hill were **one zone with no boundary between them**. No block boundary means no train can be in the station while another is on the lift, which is the entire reason a real ride puts one there. Physics are identical to a block brake (the same pair of authorities, the same `MakeLift`); the separate enumerator exists for the boundary, exactly as `BlockBrake`'s does. Both presets gain a block; the two-train circuit is unchanged at 8 blocks, 5 holding, 4 trains, because its station and launch were already different kinds. **Appended, never inserted** — `ETUSegmentZone` is a `uint8` UENUM serialised into every level that has a track in it, so inserting in the middle would silently renumber every zone already authored. A level saved before this keeps its station as `Lift` until the preset is reloaded.
- **Drives, so a command and an outcome can disagree** (`Prototypes/BlockSignal/TrackDrives.h`). The mirror of the sensor layer, and it closes the same kind of hole one side over: sensors were the PLC's inputs, and the *output* side was still a lie — `ServeHolds` wrote a speed straight into `FTrain`, the track was at that speed the same instant, and there was nothing to disagree with and nothing to report back. A **VFD is the muscle for one motor**: it takes a speed command, ramps its output toward it, and turns a tyre or a chain at whatever that means in m/s. Writing a command is now **the whole of the PLC's authority over the ride** — how fast the drive gets there is the drive's business. One drive per zone, indexed the same way, so a friction-only trim brake keeps its slot with no motor in it. **Three numbers that can disagree**, each meaning something different: `Commanded ≠ Output` is ramping, `Output ≠ Actual` is slipping, `Load = 1` is full torque. Load costs nothing to produce — `FTrain::Step` already clamps the acceleration a zone asks for to what that zone *has*, and the clamped value is the force on the train, so reporting it is the whole of it. **A fault needs slip *and* torque *and* time *and* not gaining**, and that last term took a measurement to find: a **launch is sustained slip at full torque by definition** — 0 to 38 m/s at 6 m/s² is 6.3 seconds of the drive flat out against a train nowhere near its output speed — so slip, torque and time alone report a healthy launch as a failure, and did; the two-train circuit faulted **drive 1, its launch, on the first dispatch**. What separates a launch from a stall is not how big the gap is or how long it lasts but whether it is **closing**. Removing the term still fails that assertion, so the guard is confirmed to bite. A fault is **reported, never acted on** — a real VFD trips its output and coasts, but what a ride does about a failed drive is an E-stop policy and the PLC's decision, not a property of the motor. Ramping defaults to **off**, because every number measured before drives existed was measured with output equal to command and a default that ramped would silently move all of them; a ramp only changes anything if it is *slower* than the zone's grip. One thing it fixed on the way past: zones live on each `FTrain`, so every train carried its own private idea of what every brake on the ride was doing — a drive is one device, and its output goes to every train's copy each frame. The tick is now the IEC 61131-3 scan end to end: read every switch once, run the dispatcher for every train against that one snapshot, ramp the drives, write the outputs, step the world, read the motors back. Interleaving the program with the physics is what a game does and what a PLC cannot.
- **The stop mark is a switch, not a sum.** Each zone now carries a **stop mark** — a proximity switch at `deviceEnd − HoldNoseClearanceM` — and the dispatcher's parking rule becomes *truck forward until the mark trips.* `ServeHolds` previously computed `zoneEnd − clearance − ½trainLength` and compared it against `GetDistance()`: two quantities a real PLC has neither of. Train length is now consumed **once, where the mark is placed**, which is where an installer with a tape measure consumes it, and the holding logic downstream reads a boolean. The margin stops being a number in the program and becomes a position on the track. Measured at all five holding devices on the two-train circuit: the mark sits **1.00 m** short of the block end, the nose parks **0.18 m** past the mark, and **0.81 m** of the metre survives — identical at every device, because the overshoot is `v²/2a` at the crawl speed and the grip, and both are uniform. That uniformity is what makes the overshoot safe to design around, and it also names the number to watch: a crawl above **3.46 m/s** would eat the whole metre. Both stages stay visible — the mid-course pad puts the train down at 926.4 m and the tyres truck it a further **67.3 m** to its mark. The mark trips on the **nose**, the same edge asymmetry the block counter runs on. It can be tripped by *any* train, because a switch has no idea which, and is unambiguous only because the interlocking guarantees the train at this device is the only one that can be in this block — the interlocking and the identity tracking hold each other up. Fails **closed**: no sensor list, or one that disagrees with the zone list about how many devices there are, means the device is not told to go. Inputs are scanned **once at the top of the frame**, before any logic runs, which is what IEC 61131-3 means by *read inputs → execute → write outputs* and is why the second train is served against the same snapshot as the first.
- **A held train parks its nose a metre short of the block end** (`HoldNoseClearanceM`), not in the middle of the device. Measured from the END and applied to the NOSE, because what the margin prevents is the train protruding into the next zone — a lift, a launch, open course — through a defect or a mistake; the margin is the reason the number exists, so it is expressed as the margin. Mid-device was only ever the minimum fix for the seam straddle, and is arbitrary everywhere else: on the 130 m mid-course brake it parked a train 65 m in with 65 m of empty brake ahead of it, where a real one holds near the exit. The brake does not put the train there — it stops it well short, wherever the pad lands it, and the drive tyres truck it the rest of the way. Measured, both stages visible: the mid-course pad stops the train 54 m into its block and the tyres convey it a further **67.1 m**; every device parks its nose 1.1 m from the end, the extra 0.1 being the crawl dead band.
- **Anti-rollback** (`FTUTrackSegment::bAntiRollback`, `FTrain::AddAntiRollback`). The most universal safety device on a coaster — ratchets, chain dogs, a catch car, on every lift hill ever built — and until now the model had none, so the one failure mode the physics can actually produce had nothing standing in front of it: `bAllowRollback` simulated a train running backwards and nothing stopped it. It is deliberately **not a zone**: a zone is a *control* device with a speed, an authority and something commanding it every frame, where a catch has none of those, works by being passive, and **overlaps** zones freely because a lift hill is a powered run and a ratchet at the same time. It is also **not a block boundary**, because it cannot release a train — no more a place to park one than a trim brake is. A caught train is **arrested**, not merely stopped from moving: throwing away the backward advance alone would leave it pinned in place still carrying its backward velocity, which is not a state any hardware can be in, so the dog takes the energy into the structure. Engagements are counted on the **rising edge**, because "the catch engaged once" is a number that means the same thing at any timestep and "engaged for 56,685 steps" is not. `FRideProfile::bCaughtByAntiRollback` reports it as a **third outcome**: a stall says the hill is too tall, a rollback says that *and* that the train is loose the wrong way, a catch says the hill is too tall **and the safety device did its job**. The two-train preset fits it over the launch and the whole climb, where a real launched coaster puts it, and it changes nothing at all while the ride works — which is exactly the property a safety device should have.
- **A layout carries one fewer train than it has places to stand**, and trains are placed **mid-device** rather than at a device's start. Two more things only findable by running more trains than anyone had. With five holding places, four trains run clean and **five never move at all** — every train parked exactly where the train behind it needs to go, no violation, no code-level deadlock, the ride simply full. So the cap is `holding places − 1`, not the number of places (which permitted the gridlock) and not `blocks/(1 + lookahead)` (neither necessary nor sufficient here — it allows the four that collided at lookahead 1, and forbids the three that run at lookahead 2). The fifth train also arrived **already in violation before moving a metre**: placed at its zone's *start*, a 15 m train hangs back over the boundary, and for the station — whose start is the circuit's seam — that overlaps whatever stands in the last block. Mid-device placement is both where the dispatcher parks a held train and the only placement that fits a whole train inside one block.
- **The dispatch permissive derives its braking distance instead of guessing it** (`FRideSignals::SetHoldingBlocks`). `CanDispatch(FromBlock, Lookahead)` cleared a *count* of blocks — a guess at a distance, and on a layout whose free runs are **696 m and 184 m** no single count is right for both. Measured with the count rule on the closed circuit: 1–3 trains clean, **four trains collide** — 14 violations at lookahead 1, 18 at lookahead 2 — always the same shape, granted a block with no device in it, therefore committed, and the block beyond it occupied on arrival. The fix supplies one bool per block (can a device here stop a train *and* let it go) and clears **from the destination to the next block that can hold the train**, however many that is: a train let into an empty block is committed until it reaches somewhere it can stop. All four then run clean. The list is optional — without it the old fixed count is used unchanged. One consequence: the rules stack, so `DispatchLookahead` becomes **extra headway rather than the safety requirement** and its default drops 2 → 1; at 2 the same four trains spend their time held, because a ring this tight cannot afford the stopping margin twice.
- **A held train brakes to a position, not to zero wherever it is** — and this was not cosmetic. A zone says *reach this speed*, and zero is reachable at once, so a train commanded to hold stopped within **0.3 m of the zone's start**. The station's start *is* the circuit's seam, so that left the back half of the train in the **last** block and a dwelling train held two. Measured consequence: **three trains deadlock**, each denied by the tail of the one in front — `train0` holding 7 and 0 wants 3, `train1` in 3 wants 5, `train2` holding 5 and 6 wants 7 — with nothing moving, nothing reported and `Violations()` reading 0. A circuit of fail-closed rules doing exactly what it was told. The fix needed **no new authored concept**: the dispatcher already commands the target every frame, so it commands `sqrt(2·a·d)` — the fastest a train may travel and still stop in `d` — which eases it down and parks it mid-device. A 15 m train centred in a 26 m station spans 5.5–20.5 m, wholly inside its own block. Asserted directly rather than via the deadlock.
- **Trains run real laps.** `FTrainConfig::bCircuit` wraps arc length instead of clamping, so on a closed layout a train drives through the seam and into the station under its own power rather than stopping at the end and being teleported back. It is **measured, never authored**: the actor checks position, heading *and* roll at the seam on every rebuild and only wraps a layout that passes, because wrapping an open one invents continuity across whatever gap is there. The other three presets keep the teleport, correctly. `S -= TotalLength` turned out to be one of four changes, and the other three each fail silently: **distance travelled** cannot be read back off the position (`S1 - S0` is negative by a lap on the crossing step, reversing that step's gravity and resistance work); **`AdvanceFrom` walks backwards** across the seam, so crossing samples re-evaluate from the start instead — which is why the seam has to close in heading and roll and not merely in position, or it pops once a lap in the same place; and **a reversed rear/front pair changes meaning** — on a strip it is a caller mistake and gets sorted, on a circuit it is a train *straddling the seam*, and sorting it claims every block between them, which is the whole ring, from one train, with no violation reported. `FRideSignals` now walks a possibly-wrapped range through a single helper so the entry test, the exit release and the occupancy query cannot disagree about what "the range" is; three mutations confirm each guard bites. Measured on the closed preset: two trains, ten minutes, **4 and 5 laps**, zero violations, never a shared block, ~24,000 frames apiece genuinely spanning the seam.
- **The two-train layout is a closed circuit.** It used to close in *height* and nothing else — ending **373.794 m** from the station pointing **86.421°** wrong, because its two turns summed to **446.42°** where a circuit needs 360 — so the train ran off the end and was teleported back. It now closes to **0.000000 m of position, 0.000084° of heading and 0.000000° of roll**, which makes that teleport invisible. **The fix is a shape, not a solver:** an oval closes analytically when both turns are the same way, exactly 180°, same radius, same easement — the lateral displacements (+2R, −2R) cancel, the along-track ones cancel, heading sums to 360, and one scalar condition remains, that the return leg's horizontal extent equals the two collinear outbound legs'. A 180° turn with easements of length E needs `arc = πR − E`. No Gauss-Newton, no freedoms, no residual. The interesting part is what that condition *costs*: **every metre spent outbound is paid for twice**, so the obvious layout — whole hill outbound, a turnaround at each end — came to 1717 m and could not be rescued, because softening the launch crest to tame the G *lengthens* it (crest length is angle ÷ curvature), which lengthens the circuit, which costs more energy than a longer launch buys. Measured: every variant stalled, at 914, 930, 954, 1470 and 1574 m. Splitting the hill across the turn — a top-hat turnaround, as real launched coasters do — is cheaper in three currencies at once: the train is slowest at the top so the turn costs least lateral G, a small radius suffices there, and the drop's horizontal extent lands in the return leg where the closure condition needs it. Closing it improved nearly everything, because the constraints pulled the same way: lateral G **0.54 → 0.15**, self-clearance **7.75 → 11.68 m**, roll-rate diagnostics **4 → 0** (peak 17.4 °/s), holding blocks **3 → 5**, same 136.8 km/h top speed, vertical −0.53..+3.08 against −0.35..+3.57, over 1288 m instead of 1072. Two numbers had to be hunted rather than solved: the first closed layout that completed crawled its turnaround at **2.70 m/s** — a 37-second dead spot — fixed by lowering the crest 57.3 → 48.5 m for **12.14 m/s**; and roll rate scales cleanly with easement (34.0, 25.8, 20.8, 17.4 °/s at 26, 34, 42, 50 m), so the easement is 50.
- **The mid-course brake became a real block brake, as a side effect of closing the circuit.** It was recorded as a trim that could never hold — 28.19 m/s arriving, 66.2 m needed, 45 m available. The return leg had to grow to satisfy the closure condition, and that spare length went into the brake rather than into dead straight: **130 m against the 58.1 m** it needs. The circuit gained a queueing position on the far side from the station, and every one of its five holding places now passes `v²/2a` against its own block.
- **`AnalyseSelfClearance` can measure the short way round** (`bCircuit`, off by default). A closed circuit's first and last samples are the same piece of track, so a linear separation calls them `TotalLength` apart, never skips them, and reports the closure itself as a **0.00 m self-intersection** — measured, 0.00 m at S=0.0 against S=1717.0 with nothing wrong anywhere. Off by default because a point-to-point layout's two ends genuinely are far apart, and hiding that would be the opposite error. Both behaviours are asserted, so neither can quietly become the other.
- **Two trains run one circuit, interlocked, and a block brake finally does its job.** `FRideSignals` takes a train index; `FTrain` gained `SetZoneTargetSpeed`, `GetZoneTargetSpeed` and `FindHoldZoneAt`; the actor gained `TrainCount` and `ETUSegmentZone::BlockBrake`. Multi-train was never a widening of storage — **three reads had no notion of which train they were about**, and each failed *open*, silently. *Entry:* "was I already in this block" meant "was the last train to update already in it", so a train rolling into an occupied block matched and the only function that can report a violation never ran — a collision **suppressed**, not missed. *Exit:* releasing "the" old range meant one train walked out of another's blocks on its behalf, so a block read CLEAR with a train parked in it. *Permissive:* skipping "blocks the asking train holds" fired for whichever train updated last, granting entry into a block another train was standing in. All three are now named tests, all three confirmed to bite by **mutation** — reverting each identity check kills exactly one test and no others. The single-argument forms survive for the layouts that really do run one train and **deny** on a multi-train instance rather than aliasing to train 0. Two contracts the layer cannot enforce from inside are documented as the caller's: `Tick` is once per **frame**, not once per train, or a 5 s overlap expires in 5/N seconds; and update order within a frame is observable but **fails closed**.
- **Holding a train is done by gating its zone, not by declining to integrate it** — so a station stops being a special case and the same four lines hold a train anywhere there is a device to hold it. That makes "where may a train be held" a real question with two measured parts. **The device needs both authorities:** a friction brake can stop a train and never start one, a launch can start one and never stop one, and only a drive-tyre run has both — so `FindHoldZoneAt` refuses the other two outright, because a gated launch is an *aborted* launch and a train parked on a trim brake stands there for the session with no symptom but a ride that quietly stopped. **The block needs the length:** `v²/2a` against the block, which no zone kind can change. Measured on the two-train preset at the fixed 6 m/s² grip — the mid-course brake receives a train at **28.19 m/s** and would need **66.2 m** inside a **45 m** block, so it stays a trim; the three pre-station devices need 4.8, 1.5 and 0.9 m and are the ones that hold. Authoring the mid-course one as a block brake would build a device that closes and is then run straight through, which is *worse* than a trim because it looks like an interlock. Together these cap train count at the number of places that can both hold a train and release it — four on the two-train preset, one on every other — and asking for more is refused with a log line rather than granted onto open course. Holding devices rest **closed**: a device that opens because nobody is asking is a device that fails open.
- **`Prototypes/TrainPhysics/test_twotrains.cpp`** — the only file crossing all three layers, and the first place a block brake can demonstrate itself, since with one train nothing is ever ahead of it. On the real preset geometry it proves the outer pre-station brake **stops** a moving train (7.64 m/s to 0 in 4.7 m), **holds** it stopped *and red* for 3.88 s while the block ahead is occupied — a different claim from merely stopped, which a stall would also satisfy — and **releases** it once the train ahead clears. Its last test is a line-for-line stand-in for `ATUCoasterRide::Tick`, which exists because that function cannot be compiled without Unreal and its dispatch policy has nowhere else to be checked: ten minutes of ride, **5 and 6 laps**, no deadlock, no shared block, zero violations. Deadlock is the specific thing it hunts — every rule in the policy fails closed, and a circuit of fail-closed rules is exactly the shape that stops for ever with nothing reporting anything wrong.
- **The project wears its own identity** — splash screen and application icon, generated from the brand pack. `Content/Splash/Splash.bmp` and `EdSplash.bmp` at **720×370**, `IconDefault.bmp` / `EdIconDefault.bmp` at **64×64**, and a multi-resolution `Build/Windows/Application.ico`. Those sizes are what UE 5.8 actually ships in `Engine/Content/Splash`, checked rather than taken from documentation. No config entries are needed: `FGenericPlatformSplash::GetSplashPath` looks in the project's content directory before falling back to the engine's, and flags a project splash as custom — which is why UE does not overlay the project name on artwork that already carries the wordmark. The splash is a crop of the reference-layout drawing rather than the whole sheet shrunk: at 720×370 the full drawing is an illegible smear, whereas the lockup, the segment-vocabulary table and the lift curve all still read.
- **Brand and promo pack** — wordmark, lockup, mark, badge, avatar and favicon as standalone SVG, plus a brand-system page, an animated loading screen, a site and a social-card set. **Since removed from the codebase**: only the five figures the README and `Docs/` embed are tracked, under `Brand/github/`, and the rest is gitignored. The measurement driver that lived alongside it was never branding and stayed — it is `Prototypes/TrackSpline/reference_figures.cpp`, and it is what makes `REFERENCE_LAYOUT.md`'s figures reproducible.
- **A train can roll back** (`FTrainConfig::bAllowRollback`, off by default). `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` recorded stopping dead as a deliberate Phase 0 limitation; the fix turns out to be smaller than it implies, because the position integration already computed a negative advance on a gradient and threw it away. Removing that clamp is most of it. The rest is that resistance is charged over the **distance covered** rather than over signed travel — multiplied by a signed displacement, friction becomes an energy *source* the moment a train rolls backwards, and it would fail in the most flattering possible way, as a train returning faster than it left. Asserted that way round: frictionless, out at **15.000 m/s** and back past the same point at **15.000 m/s**; with resistance, **12 reversals** before settling 0.8 m from the valley's true bottom. Off by default because stopping dead is what every earlier number was measured against and one test asserts it directly — and because a valley stall is a design error to *surface*, where an oscillation can read as the ride working. `FRideProfile` therefore reports a rollback **distinctly from** a stall: same location, different diagnosis, because a rollback also means the train is loose on the track heading the wrong way. `GetSpeed()` still returns a magnitude so nothing written before this changed meaning; `GetVelocity()` is the signed one.
- **Three cameras, switchable mid-ride with `C`** — rider, chase, free. A Details-panel property cannot change view while the train is moving, and switching view while riding is the whole point. **Chase** holds level with the world: the old non-ride view took the *rider's* rotation and stepped back from it, so it turned upside down through the loop, which made the inversion the hardest part of the ride to look at. It smooths in world space with one exponential follow, because a rigid offset reads as a camera bolted to the train and lagging it is what makes speed legible. **Free** flies with WASD / Q / E / mouse / Shift, seeds from wherever the camera already was so switching in does not teleport you, and pins roll to zero — a free camera that rolls is a lost camera. The ride carries on without you, which is the point. Controls are shown on screen, because a keybinding nobody knows about does not exist. Direct key bindings rather than Enhanced Input: its real advantages — rebinding, modifiers, layered contexts — only arrive with content assets to hold them, and creating those transiently in code buys the machinery without the benefit. That is a Phase 6 concern and is marked as one.
- **You can pick a seat** (`ATUCoasterRide::RiderPosition`, −1 back row to +1 front). The camera and the on-screen G readout both follow it, so riding the back row is a thing the sim can now actually offer rather than a claim about real coasters. And the train renders as one car per sample point instead of a single cube, so its length is visible on the track — instanced, sharing one mesh, with frames taken from the train rather than from `EvaluateAt`, which is O(track length) a call and would otherwise be six of those every frame.
- **The vertical slice rides a 15 m train** (`ATUCoasterRide::TrainLengthM`), and the on-screen readout shows what the front and back cars each feel. Measured against the point mass on this layout: peak vertical **+4.30 → +4.20 g**, because a long train does not pay the full depth of the loop's bottom, and the loop apex **firms from +1.34 to +1.52 g**, because straddling the top means more speed there. Both directions are what a real designer expects — length softens sharp features and steadies inversions.
- **The train has length** (`FTrainConfig::TrainLength`). `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` called a point-mass train "the one omission most likely to be *felt*", and predicted the fix would be an average over sample points along the train — which is what it is, nine of them. The substitution that matters is one line: the gravity term reads the change in the train's **mean** height rather than its centre's, because a rigid body on rails is accelerated by the slope its whole mass sits on. Measured: a point mass crests at **19.619 m/s** where a 15 m train arrives at **19.735 m/s**, because straddling the crest its mass sits below the crest and it has not paid the full height. Across an asymmetric crest the spread front-to-back is **0.73 g** — front car −1.996 g, centre −2.318 g, back car −2.723 g. `TrainLength = 0` stays the default and collapses every sample to one, so every result measured before this is bit-identical, asserted exactly rather than within a tolerance. `GetForcesAt(offset)` reports what any car feels; `GetFrontS`/`GetRearS` are there for the block system to use later. **One condition the findings did not state: the back-car effect requires an *asymmetric* hill** — on a symmetric one the front and back crest at the same height and there is no difference at all, which the first version of the test correctly measured. Both cases are asserted, the symmetric one as a control.
- **The whole ride, measured at edit time** (`Prototypes/TrainPhysics/RideProfile.h`), drawn as toggleable traces along the track. Runs the train once over the finished layout and records speed, all three felt-G axes, roll rate and height, sampled by **arc length** rather than by time so the data does not thin out exactly where the ride is fastest and the G is worth looking at. Extremes carry *where*: "4.25 g" is a number, "4.25 g at S=310 m" is somewhere to go and look. The single most useful thing it reports is `bCompleted` — an author needs to know a hill is too tall **before** watching a train fail to crest it, and the only way to know is to run it. Each channel draws as a curve offset from the heartline along the rider's own up-vector, so the track is the zero line and through an inversion the trace goes with the rider. Roll rate is a first-class channel because no G trace can ever show it: felt G models the rider as a point, and spinning a point costs nothing. Per-channel visibility and one scale for now — the panel with units and readouts is a UI/UX design question and wants designing rather than accreting.
- **The track is editable.** `Source/TrackUnlimited/TUTrackSegment.h` reflects the authored segment into Unreal, and `ATUCoasterRide` now holds a `TArray<FTUTrackSegment>` seeded with the reference layout — so the ride is *data in the Details panel* rather than a sequence of `AddSegment` calls only editable by recompiling. **The Details panel is the numeric entry UI**: it already has typed fields, per-kind visibility via `EditCondition`, add/remove/reorder, copy/paste, multi-select and its own undo, and reimplementing that in Slate would be a great deal of code to arrive back where we started. It also satisfies the first architectural constraint exactly — numbers in a list, no drag handles anywhere near the viewport. Powered track is per segment too (`ETUSegmentZone`), so a lift is however many segments in a row say "Lift" and moving one is an edit rather than a recompile; arc lengths accumulate over the segments the track actually *accepted*, so a rejected degenerate segment cannot silently shift every zone boundary after it. Every edit rebuilds and reports total length, C² continuity, height gap and closest self-approach — each of which was a defect that previously took *riding* the track to find.
- **Undo/redo** (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackHistory.h`). Snapshots, not commands: the command pattern needs every edit to supply a do *and* an inverse, and the failure mode when those disagree is silent corruption found several undos later. A document is an ordered list of small POD structs, so copying it whole costs nothing and cannot be asymmetric. Equality goes through the **save format** rather than a hand-written `operator==` — correct semantics (two documents are the same exactly when they save the same) and it cannot rot, which matters because `Torsion` and `RollMode` were both added to this model after the fact and a field-by-field comparison would have silently ignored them and dropped undo steps. It also makes a field the segment's kind does not use correctly compare equal, since it is not part of the document's meaning. Committing an unchanged document is a no-op, so an editor that fires on every field callback does not fill the stack with steps that undo nothing. Typing "30.5" into a field is one undo step, not five, via a caller-supplied merge key — the UI owns it because only the UI knows when an edit is finished, so there are no timers and no guessing. Dirty state is tracked as a **position** rather than a flag, so undoing back to what is on disk correctly reports clean; if the depth cap discards the saved state it reports dirty forever, because telling someone their work is safe when the evidence was thrown away is the wrong direction to be wrong in.
- **Self-clearance checking** (`AnalyseSelfClearance` in `Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackValidate.h`), and `ChainCurvature` in `TrackSpline.h`. Nothing in the model could see the track passing through itself, and it took *riding* the vertical slice to find out: its loop is built from pure pitch curvature, which makes it exactly planar, so the ascending and descending legs pass **0.189 m** apart against rails **1.215 m** wide. Every segment individually valid, every joint continuous, every G reading plausible, rider through solid steel. Third member of the same family — felt G cannot see roll rate, the authored list cannot see the roll geometry contributes, and neither can see two parts of the track in the same place. Fixing it needs a lateral component, i.e. torsion, and composing torsion across segments needed `ChainCurvature`: the phase is measured from each segment's own start, so three torsioned segments in a row step at every joint. Measured cost of the fix on the slice, recorded rather than applied because it is a ride-design call: torsion 0.003 opens the gap to **2.39 m** but takes peak lateral G from **0.36 to 0.96** and drops the exit **22.3 m**.
- **Per-axis closure targets** (`FClosureTarget::bMatchX/Y/Z`, and `HeightTarget`). "Closed" is not one question. A circuit wants all three axes; a point-to-point layout — which is most of what exists right now — wants only Z, because a station-to-brake-run ride is not supposed to end back at the station's footprint. Held to a full circuit target the vertical slice reports 265 m of perfectly truthful, entirely useless failure with the one number that matters buried inside it. Convergence is judged on `ActiveError`, over only the axes the target asked about.
- **Circuit closure: measuring the gap, and solving it** (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackClose.h`). `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` calls endpoint targeting "the one real cost of the representation" — you author curvature and arrive wherever you arrive, where a control-point model gets closure free. `MeasureClosure` reports the gap per axis and gives **height its own field**, because that is the one that does not announce itself: a track ending 8.5 m low still looks closed in plan view, which is how the vertical slice shipped. `SolveClosure` is damped Gauss-Newton over **authored** parameters the caller explicitly frees — so a solved arc keeps the radius the author typed instead of gaining a curvature field that disagrees with it, and nothing moves that the author did not offer. Measured: 9.093 m closes to 3.0e-06 m in 2 iterations and 11 track walks; 20.98 m to 7.0e-06 m in 2. Radius freedoms have their **sign locked and magnitude floored**, because +ve is a left turn and -ve a right one, so crossing zero would reverse a turn and pass through the infinite curvature that produces a silently straight segment. The finite-difference step is deliberately coarse, because the standing "do not finite-difference `EvaluateAt`" warning applies to the solver too. On failure the document is left **unchanged** by default. Two limits are measured rather than fixed: horizontal straights cannot move height at all (8.93 m survives a solve untouched, zero gradient), and freeing one half of a balanced hill breaks the balance that made it level — closing height while keeping the exit level needs coupled freedoms this solver cannot express.
- **The authored data model and a diffable save format** (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackIO.h`). `FAuthoredSegment` stores what the author *typed* — a helix is "radius 20, climbing 15°, 1.5 turns", not the "length 261.6, curvature 0.0467, torsion 0.0125" the integrator runs — and JSON round-trips it. The rule is **store what was typed, never what was derived**, and it is the same argument that justified the torsion field: the approximation a helix would otherwise need costs roughly one segment per metre, rides identically, and contains the radius **nowhere**, so it can never be edited again. Grouping those segments in the UI fixes the row count and not that — to edit the group you must regenerate it, so the group has to store the authored parameters anyway, at which point the expansion is a cache and this stores the parameters alone. Measured on the format itself: a helix radius change is **one line of diff**; the same edit against a stored expansion would rewrite ~600 fitted curvature endpoints, and only if regeneration were bit-deterministic across compilers. Round trip is **exact** — worst position error 0.0 m over a six-kind layout — and re-saving a loaded file is byte-identical, so opening and closing a track cannot churn a diff. **Angles are degrees** — in the file and in `FAuthoredSegment` itself, the only place in the codebase that is not radians. Nobody types `0.26179938779914941`; they type `15`, and a diff that reads `"climbAngleDeg": 15` says what changed where the radian form says nothing. Putting degrees in the *struct* rather than converting in the reader is what keeps the round trip exact: the conversion runs one direction only, in `BuildSegment`, so degrees → radians → degrees never happens and cannot lose an ulp. Field and key names carry the unit so a hand edit cannot get it wrong. Curvature stays 1/m, because an easement out of a straight has an endpoint with no radius, and curvature 0 says that as an ordinary value where radius would need infinity. Defaults are omitted rather than written, which is what let `Torsion` and `RollMode` both be added to an existing model without touching a stored line. Unknown *keys* load and are ignored (forward compatibility); an unknown segment **kind** or a future format version is refused outright, because silently skipping geometry leaves a file that loads, looks fine, and is a different ride. 11 malformed inputs rejected with a reason, and the writer refuses to emit a non-finite value rather than write `nan`.
- **World-referenced roll, as a per-segment mode** (`ERollMode` in `Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackSpline.h`). `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` recorded that `Roll = 0` does not mean level with the horizon on non-planar track, and left "keep rotation-minimising" versus "add a world-referenced mode" open as a Phase 1 decision. The answer is both, per segment, because neither is defined everywhere a coaster goes: world bank has no meaning pointing straight up, and path-relative roll makes the author compute their own banking. A corner hill authored at roll 0 exits **54.736°** off level path-relative and **5.7e-14** world-referenced. Resolved per sample at walk time rather than baked in when typed, so editing an upstream hill cannot silently unbank a turn downstream; `FTrackFrame::Roll` stays path-relative, so felt G, the cross-section and the ride camera needed no changes at all. Cheap because roll never perturbs the path — it is applied last, as a rotation about the tangent, making the solve one `atan2` against a frame already in hand.
- **`AnalyseResolvedRollRate`** (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackValidate.h`), which walks the built track rather than reading the authored list — and closes a second blind spot found while adding the mode above. A segment holding a constant world bank through a climbing turn types **0.0 rad/m** of roll rate and rotates the rider the whole way, at a measured **0.0296 rad/m** (34.0 °/s, 0.018 g of head snap), because the path frame twists underneath the constant number. Felt G cannot see roll rate; the segment list cannot see geometry. It also covers world bank's failure mode without a special case: the level reference flips through the vertical, so holding a bank over the top of a loop measures **12.6 rad/m** against **0.0** for identical geometry authored path-relative.
- **Vertical slice** (`Source/TrackUnlimited/TUCoasterRide.cpp`). The first code to take the prototypes into the engine, and the last Phase 0 engineering item. A hand-authored 543.7 m layout — station, eased 25° lift, crest, drop, teardrop loop, banked turn, brake run — authored numerically as an ordered list of typed segment parameters, with no viewport dragging anywhere. `Build.cs` puts `Prototypes/` on the public include path and includes the headers **directly** rather than copying them, so the standalone assert suites test exactly the code that ships. Builds clean against UE 5.8.1 with zero warnings, and in-engine `BuildTrack` logs the same `16 segments, 543.7 m, curvature-continuous=yes` the standalone harness produces. Cart rides the rail centreline, camera rides the heartline, so the slice shows the distinction rather than collapsing it.
- **NoLimits 2 CSV import/export prototype** (`Prototypes/NL2Csv/`). Reads and writes NL2's documented tab-separated spline export, so real NL2 layouts can be driven through the model and ours opened in NL2 for comparison. Reconstruction fits curvature segments from sampled frames by decomposing each interval's Darboux vector against a parallel-transported path frame. Round-trips a 529.8 m layout to 2.6 mm worst position and 6.8e-04 g rms felt-G, with a full-circuit train speed divergence of 1.7e-03 m/s. Validated against a real 466-sample NL2 export. 13 assert-based tests.
- **NoLimits 2 telemetry prototype** (`Prototypes/NL2Telemetry/`). The NL2 telemetry wire protocol as an engine-free, socket-free header (testable offline), plus a live recorder that correlates a running ride against an imported track. Telemetry ships in the **standard** NL2 edition, unlike the Professional-only CSV export, so this path works regardless of licence tier. 7 assert-based tests against hand-built bytes.
- **Train physics prototype** (`Prototypes/TrainPhysics/`). 1D train motion constrained to the track, standalone C++17. Gravity is applied as an exact energy exchange rather than an integrated force, so a frictionless circuit conserves energy to 5e-14 m/s across a 333× range of timestep. Rolling resistance follows the normal load from the track's felt G, air drag is a lumped `v²` term, and one zone shape covers lift chain, tyre launch, brake run and station — they differ only in target speed and in how hard they may push or hold back. Reports fore-aft G alongside the track's lateral and vertical. 18 assert-based tests, 22 of 22 mutants killed.
- **Track spline prototype** (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/`). Curvature-continuous track geometry as standalone C++17 with no engine dependency. Segments are curvature profiles over arc length — straight, constant-radius arc, clothoid transition — integrated into geometry by an exponential-midpoint scheme that keeps the frame orthonormal by construction. Heartline-relative banking, rail centreline derivation, and felt lateral/vertical G. 14 assert-based tests.
- **Block signalling prototype** (`Prototypes/BlockSignal/`). The `CLEAR → OCCUPIED → BUFFER(x) → CLEAR` per-block state machine with configurable safety overlap, plus dispatch permissive logic with wrapping multi-block lookahead. Standalone C++17, 7 assert-based tests.
- **`Docs/PHASE0_FINDINGS.md`** — what the prototypes proved (with numbers), what was disproved, the known-limitations ledger, and the measured UE5 port checklist.
- UE5 project skeleton: `TrackUnlimited.uproject`, `Config/`, and an empty `TrackUnlimited` runtime module.
- This changelog.

### Changed

- **Phase 4's procedural meshing layer will be built, not adapted — the Coaster Forge evaluation is closed, and with it the last Phase 0 gate.** `PROJECT_PLAN.md` Section 8 called licensing/adapting existing marketplace work "the single biggest lever available to compress a multi-year solo effort", but that lever does not exist here: Coaster Forge is a commercial product and cannot be redistributed under MIT, which the repo's own constraint on paid marketplace assets already implied. The purchase would have bought reference reading followed by an original implementation regardless. Two things also moved since the plan was written — the zone-based speed control cited as the pattern worth borrowing was arrived at independently and verified against NoLimits 2 (`FTrackZone`), and a cross-section specification now exists (`Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackProfile.h`), so Phase 4 begins from typed dimensions rather than a blank sheet. What remains there is technique, chiefly support/footer auto-placement, which no prior art was going to de-risk.
- **The lateral-G sign now matches a running copy of NoLimits 2, and the documentation reading that contradicted it was wrong.** `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` flagged this as "one negation now and expensive later" and it turns out to be **no negation at all**: across a recorded lap, sign agreement is 627/627 samples wherever NL2's lateral exceeds 0.3 g. Magnitude agreement is far tighter than the sign check needed — 0.0089 g rms vertical and 0.0299 g rms lateral over 4,298 samples, with the peak unbanked turn reading +2.3521 g in NL2 against +2.3979 g in the model. NL2 was also shown to report G in the **car's local frame** (0.909 on the lift against cos(pitch) = 0.914, versus 1.000 for world) and as the force felt **by the rider**, both decided from data rather than from the docs, which specify neither.
- **`RollingResistance` and `DragK` have their first real measurement.** Fitted to NL2's own speed trace over 1,700 coasting samples, the shipped defaults run that ride 0.998 m/s rms fast; 0.0046 / 0.00144 halves it to 0.564. **The defaults are deliberately unchanged** — one train on one 233 m layout, and the fitted `DragK` lands 3.2× above its physically derived value, which is what modelling a 10–15 m train as a point at the heartline would look like. Recorded as evidence for the train-length limitation, not as a new default.
- **Track representation is curvature-profile-over-arc-length, superseding the cubic Hermite / B-spline model** originally specified in `PROJECT_PLAN.md` Section 5. C² continuity becomes a property of the data rather than something fitted, and clothoids are the native case rather than special-cased. Rationale in `Prototypes/TrackSpline/TrackSpline.h`; consequences in `Docs/PHASE0_FINDINGS.md`.
- `PROJECT_PLAN.md` Section 6 no longer describes the Phase 1 editor as having "draggable handles" — that contradicted the project's first architectural constraint, and the data model has no control points to drag.
- The segment vocabulary now records that **a helix is not expressible as a linear curvature profile** and needs its own segment type. Authored naively it produces a flat tilted circle, not a helix.
- `README.md`, `CONTRIBUTING.md` and `CLAUDE.md` updated for a repo that now contains working code, including build instructions for the prototypes and the UE 5.8 engine pin.

### Fixed

- **`RollingResistance` was justified against the wrong material, and is now measured.** The old 0.006 was documented as "a plausible steel-on-steel figure" — but steel wheel on steel rail is a *railway*. Coaster running wheels are **polyurethane** on steel, and the hysteresis loss as that elastomer deforms under load is a real, continuous drain. Published polyurethane-on-steel figures sit around **0.01–0.03**; steel-on-steel is nearer 0.001–0.002, so the old default was not correct even for the material it named. Corrected to **0.024** on the strength of three NoLimits 2 recordings across two coaster types and two layouts, all fitting **0.022–0.026** — the cleanest being a purpose-built 621 m dead-flat coast-down launched at exactly 30 km/h, fitting `Crr = 0.02602` with a **0.0005 m/s² residual** against decelerations of 0.1–0.2. That 0.3% fit says the model's *shape* was right all along and only this constant was wrong, and it reproduced bit-identically across two recordings of the same configuration. It also resolves something previously recorded as inexplicable: rolling resistance outweighing air drag 3:1 even at 100 km/h — absurd for a steel railway wheel, exactly right for polyurethane tyres. `DragK` stays at its derived 0.00045: the fit prefers 0.0001, but at 30 km/h drag is a minority term the data cannot really see, and a derived value beats a weakly determined one.
- **The vertical slice re-tuned for the corrected physics.** At 0.024 its loop crested at **+0.13 g** — hanging on the track rather than held to it. The lever is the drop, not the lift: **12 m → 24 m** restores **+1.13 g** and the original profile (105.2 km/h, +0.66..+4.19 g). Raising the lift changes no G number at all on this layout — measured across 75–115 m, because the chain delivers the train to the crest at 4 m/s whatever the height — so the lift went **75.11 m → 90.99 m** purely to close the ending back to station level, which deepening the drop had put 6.710 m low.

- **The vertical slice no longer ends 8.5 m underground.** What "dropping through the floor after the loop" actually was: the layout ended 8.498 m below its own station and ran its entire back half below ground, with the rail centreline bottoming out at **−9.60 m**. It had been worked around by raising the actor in the level rather than fixed. `TrackClose.h`'s `HeightTarget` with the lift climb freed solves it in 2 iterations — 55.0 m → **75.11 m**, ending at +0.0007 m. **It costs nothing in ride feel, and that is not luck:** the chain hauls the train to the crest at 4 m/s whatever the crest's height, and the drop geometry below it never moved, so top speed (100.9 km/h), both vertical G peaks (+0.70..+4.25), peak lateral (0.36) and the loop apex (+1.25) all come out bit-identical to the 55 m version. Only the height moved. The lift length is now one named constant used twice, because the chain zone's extent is derived from where the climb ends and a second literal could have drifted out of step and stranded the train short of the crest.
- **The vertical slice's chain was holding the train down the drop.** A regression from moving zones to per-segment granularity: the crest is authored as a *pair* of eased-pitch segments and marking both as "Lift" carried the chain 17 m past the top. `MakeLift` holds a fast train back as well as pulling a slow one, so it sat on the train at 4 m/s down a 34° drop — costing **10 km/h** of top speed (101.4 → 91.6) and flattening the loop apex from **+1.34 g to nothing**. Only the first of the pair is powered now, releasing just past where pitch crosses zero. The window is narrow in both directions: release at the top of the climb instead and the train strands, because the straight tops out while the track is still rising.
- **The recorded `RollingResistance` / `DragK` fit was never a measurement of two things, and `PHASE0_FINDINGS.md` now says so.** `Prototypes/NL2Telemetry/calibrate.cpp` refits it properly — for a coasting train the model is *linear* in both coefficients, so the whole fit is a closed-form 2×2 solve rather than the forward-simulation search the first attempt used, which is also where its two segfaults came from. The diagnostic that settles it: the correlation between the two predictors, `N·g` and `v²`, is **0.975** with a condition number around **2000**. They move together across this recording, so no fit can attribute the energy loss to one rather than the other — the split between them was a free parameter, not a result. Visible as `DragK` flipping sign (−0.00138 → +0.00143) across train lengths while the residual barely moves. The cause is the ride: it tops out at 44.5 km/h over 234 m, and drag needs speed to have anything to be measured against. **Train length does not rescue it** — that was the hypothesis and the correlation stays 0.975 at every length, so it is untested rather than disproved. What the data *does* support is one parameter: pin `DragK` at its derived 0.00045 and `RollingResistance` fits well-conditioned at **0.02204**. Defaults remain unchanged; the actionable next step is a *fast* reference recording.

- **Powered sections could manufacture energy on a gradient.** A zone was a per-*time* clamp on speed applied after a per-*distance* energy exchange, so on a slope the two ratcheted against each other: a lift chain rated at 3.0 m/s² — physically unable even to hold the train against a 30° grade's 4.9 — climbed 19.5 m anyway, inventing 191 J/kg out of nothing at every timestep tested. Zones are now tractive accelerations resolved before the energy step, so `MaxAccel` means what its comment always claimed. An underpowered lift now correctly fails to move the train, and an adequate one holds chain speed uphill at exactly `sin(grade)` G fore-aft.
- **A train at rest on a gradient never moved.** Position was integrated from entry speed alone, so zero speed meant zero travel, meant no height change, meant zero speed — forever. Placing a cart at the top of a drop, the vertical slice's most obvious first experiment, produced nothing at all. Position now carries the acceleration term, and a train released from rest reaches `sqrt(2g·Δz)` at every grade tested.
- Fore-aft G mixed conventions: lateral and vertical were apparent G, but tangential returned raw `dv/dt`, putting phantom load on every slope in the ride. A freely rolling train now reads exactly 0 G, as it should — gravity is accelerating it, not pushing against it.
- A train coming to rest could creep at ~1e-9 m/s forever, because `S1 - S0` is not bit-identical to the computed advance once the distance travelled is dwarfed by the position.
- `FTrain::AddZone` now rejects malformed zones rather than storing them — an inverted span silently never fires, and a negative `MaxDecel` turned a brake run into an unbounded launch.
- Path acceleration was resolved onto the *banked* lateral axis instead of the path's, which would have left a banked turn never quite cancelling its own lateral G — wrong in a way that throws no error.
- Roll sign was inverted: a right-hand rotation about the tangent drops the rider's right side, so `+bank` leaned *out* of a left turn.
- `FTrack::AddSegment` now rejects zero-length, negative and NaN segment lengths. A zero-length segment whose endpoint curvatures matched its neighbours could otherwise satisfy `IsCurvatureContinuous` at both joints while bridging a real discontinuity — a measured 0.76 G lateral step reported as continuous.
- `FBlockController::CanDispatch` denies a zero lookahead. It previously returned an unconditional dispatch permissive that checked nothing — verified across every state vector for N ≤ 6, including with every block occupied.
- `FBlockController::Tick` ignores zero, negative and NaN deltas. A NaN would latch a block in `BUFFER` permanently with no later well-formed tick able to free it; a negative delta grew a countdown past its configured ceiling.
- Test coverage on the vertical axis, found by mutation testing: four broken variants of the spline header passed the original suite, including one that inverted the entire track (loop apex at z = -16 instead of +16) while leaving every G reading numerically identical. All previously-surviving mutants are now killed.
